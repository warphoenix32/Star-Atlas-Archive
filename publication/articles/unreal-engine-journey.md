# The Unreal Engine Journey

For years, Star Atlas existed most vividly in trailers, concept art and the imaginations of its community. Then the doors of the Showroom opened.

Ships that had been discussed as assets, manufacturers and future instruments of play could be encountered inside a high-fidelity Unreal Engine environment. Players could move through a space built to make the promise of Galia feel physical.

That moment mattered. It also came with a precise label: **pre-alpha**.

The Unreal record contains real public access, named revisions and expanding experiments. No single release delivered the entire universe described in the project’s broader vision.

## Opening the Showroom

On September 29, 2022, ATMTA published instructions for entering the pre-alpha Showroom. The following day, an official announcement stated that the Unreal Engine 5-powered experience had launched through the Epic Games Store.

Those records provide strong evidence for the release of that named pre-alpha build. They establish that the public could access an Unreal environment and that Star Atlas presented it as a major product milestone.

They do not establish that every mechanic described in trailers, roadmaps or later website copy was present. “Pre-alpha” recorded both achievement and incompleteness. The experience had crossed from presentation into public access while remaining an early build.

The distinction is not a technical footnote. It explains why two apparently opposite statements can both be true: the Showroom genuinely launched, and the wider Star Atlas game remained unfinished.

## Ships became places

The Showroom changed the relationship between players and Star Atlas ships.

On a marketplace, a ship appears through images, specifications, price and ownership. Lore gives it a manufacturer, faction and history. The Unreal environment adds physical scale, explorable surfaces and atmosphere.

That shift helped make the project tangible. A player could understand the difference between reading that a ship was large and standing beneath it. The Showroom turned design language into spatial experience.

It also created a new evidence problem. A ship visible in one build was not automatically flyable, combat-ready or integrated into every other product. Presence, interaction, ownership and full utility are separate claims.

## R2.1 and the meaning of an update

On June 2, 2023, ATMTA announced **Showroom R2.1** as a game release connected to a racing season. The named revision is an important anchor because it proves that the public Unreal surface did not remain frozen at its first launch.

A later build can add environments, activities or systems while leaving other roadmap promises unresolved. “Updated” therefore describes the existence of a documented revision, not delivery of every future feature associated with the Unreal client.

The Library follows a build-qualified rule: a feature belongs to the build in which it is documented unless later evidence establishes broader availability. That prevents a 2026 description from being projected backward into 2022 and prevents an early pre-alpha label from erasing later progress.

## The long road beyond the Showroom

By July 12, 2026, the official Star Atlas homepage still offered an Unreal client through the Epic Games Store and described a broader range of activities, including combat, exploration, crafting, quests, racing and ship interiors.

That current-positioning snapshot demonstrates that an official Unreal access path remained part of the public product story. It does not, by itself, map each advertised feature to a particular build or prove uninterrupted availability.

This is where language matters. **UE5** names a technology family. **Showroom** names a historical product surface. Later Unreal builds may descend from or replace the Showroom, but they are not automatically aliases. An explicit build ledger or migration statement is still needed to connect every stage with confidence.

## Where C4 fits

The **C4 Public Test Realm** appears in 2026 documentation as a user-accessible testing environment with mining and other systems.

PTR access is meaningful. Players can encounter and evaluate mechanics before a wider release. But a test realm is not mainnet, and it is not proof that every system has entered general production.

C4 also should not be collapsed into the historical Showroom or the SAGE family simply because the products share ships, resources or ideas. Star Atlas products can influence one another while retaining different clients, game states and release histories.

The most defensible description is that C4 represents a later testing surface in the broader development journey. Its exact succession relationship to each earlier Unreal build remains a matter for versioned release evidence.

## Recordings as windows into development

Atlas Brew and Town Hall recordings contain years of discussion about Unreal development, ship systems and planned features. Their timestamps can tell a researcher when an idea was discussed and make it possible to return to the original video.

Those discussions need careful use. An unidentified speaker cannot support a named personal commitment. A confident roadmap statement cannot prove later release. Yet a timestamped conversation can still reveal the vocabulary, expectations and design questions surrounding a build at a particular moment.

This spoken history is most valuable alongside—not instead of—release announcements, access instructions and build notes.

## Why the journey matters

The Unreal releases document Star Atlas moving from cinematic presentation and asset ownership toward a space that players could enter.

The Showroom made that threshold visible. R2.1 demonstrated continued revision. Later access paths and C4 testing show a development story that continued to branch.

As of this review, the strongest dated findings are clear: the pre-alpha Showroom was released in September 2022; R2.1 was announced in June 2023; an official Unreal access path was present in July 2026; and C4 was documented as testing rather than general release.

A complete build history still needs to show which systems appeared, changed, moved or disappeared at every stage. Until that ledger exists, the Showroom’s documented achievements should stand on their own without absorbing every later roadmap promise.
